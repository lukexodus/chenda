A — Brand PersonalityWho Chenda Is

Chenda is not a neutral grocery app. It's a marketplace that respects perishability — treating freshness as a first-class signal, not an afterthought. The brand feels like a trusted suki at the palengke: fast, honest, and a little urgent.

1
Freshness is Felt
Time pressure isn't hidden behind badges. The visual system breathes urgency — progress bars, countdowns, and color shifts communicate shelf-life at a glance.
2
Bold, Not Loud
Typography does the heavy lifting. Syne's geometric weight creates a confident, distinct voice without resorting to gratuitous animations or cluttered UI.
3
Trust-First Commerce
Every seller has a face. Every product has provenance. Food safety signals are prominent, never buried. The buyer never has to guess.
4
Proximity as Feature
Distance is shown alongside price — not in metadata. Near things look near. Far things are filtered out naturally by the freshness-proximity model.
5
Mobile-Native Density
Information is compressed efficiently. Buyers make fast decisions. Cards carry price, freshness, distance, and seller rating without scrolling.
6
Warm, Not Corporate
Microcopy sounds like a person, not a system. "Only 2 left — grab it" beats "Low inventory detected." The brand speaks with warmth and honesty.
7
Action-Oriented
Primary actions are always visible. The user is never more than one tap from adding to cart, contacting a seller, or tracking a delivery.
8
Local Filipino Roots
Currency is ₱ Philippine Peso. Distance is km. Categories reflect local produce culture. The product feels built for the Philippine market, not adapted from it.

A — Design Principles12 Rules We Follow

1
Freshness has hierarchy
The freshness bar and status pill always appear above the price. Shelf-life is more important than cost in perishable commerce — the layout reflects this.
2
Color communicates, not decorates
Green = fresh and safe. Yellow = act soon. Red = expired or danger. These three states are sacred — never repurpose them for branding without semantic meaning.
3
One primary action per screen
Every page has a clear next step. Add to cart. Place order. Accept job. The primary CTA is full-width, green, and unmissable. Secondary actions recede.
4
Typography leads personality
Syne for display and headings — geometric, confident, unmistakably Chenda. DM Sans for body — readable, warm, functional. Never mix up the roles.
5
Distance and time are always visible
Every product card shows km and time-since-listed. Both are proximity signals. They cannot be hidden or pushed to a detail page — they inform purchase urgency.
6
States are never ambiguous
Every interactive element has a visible default, hover, active, focus, disabled, loading, error, and success state. No guessing whether a tap registered.
7
Modals and sheets are purposeful
Bottom sheets for contextual overlays (filters, quick view). Center modals for confirmations and payments. Never use a modal for content that belongs on a page.
8
Empty states earn trust
An empty cart or no results page is still a brand moment. Always include an icon, a human line of copy, and a clear action. Never show a blank screen.
9
Accessibility is not optional
Minimum 4.5:1 contrast ratio for all body text. Visible focus rings using brand green. Semantic HTML roles. Touch targets minimum 44×44px. Screen reader labels on all icons.
10
Dark mode is first-class
Not an afterthought. Every token has a dark-mode counterpart. The freshness color system stays the same — only surfaces and text adapt. Night shopping looks sharp.
11
Microcopy has a voice
Short, direct, a little warm. "Oops, we lost that page" not "Error 404." "Fresh for 2 more days" not "Expires: 2025-04-27." Write for humans in motion.
12
Roles are distinct, tokens are shared
Buyer, Seller, Rider views share the same design system — tokens, components, type scale — but navigation, data cards, and primary actions are role-tailored.

A — Interaction & MotionPrinciples

Motion should reinforce meaning — especially freshness urgency and state transitions. Never use animation as decoration.

Transitions
220ms ease
Standard for color, opacity, and shadow changes on interactive elements.
Modals & Sheets
360ms cubic-bezier(0.22,1,0.36,1)
Springy entrance for overlays. Feels physical — like lifting a card off a surface.
Freshness Bar Fill
600ms cubic-bezier(0.22,1,0.36,1)
Animate width on mount. The bar "fills" with the freshness value — it's the first data the eye reads.
Expiring Pulse
1.8s ease-in-out infinite
The "Expiring Soon" pill gently pulses. Not frantic — a soft urgency signal that doesn't distract.
Card Hover Lift
translateY(-2px) + shadow-md
Product cards lift slightly on hover, reinforcing that they're tappable objects.
Button Press
scale(0.97)
Active/press state scales down slightly. Confirms the tap registered without visual noise.
Toast Entry
translateY(-12px) + scale(0.96)
Toast slides down from under the header. Exits with fade. 4s auto-dismiss.
Skeleton Shimmer
1.6s linear infinite
Loading skeletons shimmer left-to-right. The direction implies content is "loading in."

A — Do & Don'tExamples

✓ Do
Use freshness color (green/yellow/red) consistently and only for shelf-life status
Show distance in km on every product card
Use Syne font for all headings, product names, and prices
Animate freshness bars on mount
Use bottom sheets for filters and contextual menus on mobile
Write CTA copy with active verbs: "Order Now", "Accept Job"
Keep the primary action always visible above the fold
Show seller name and verification status on every product
✗ Don't
Use green for non-freshness UI elements (e.g. decorative borders, random highlights)
Hide expiry or distance in a secondary tab or modal
Use Inter, Roboto, or system fonts — they erase brand identity
Add animations to skeleton states (they already shimmer — that's enough)
Use center modals for content that needs scrolling
Write passive CTA copy: "Submit", "Proceed", "Continue"
Stack multiple primary (green) buttons on the same screen
Show a blank screen for empty states — always provide an action

B — Freshness StatusThree-State System

Every product in Chenda exists in one of three freshness states. The visual system communicates these states through color, copy, animation, and layout hierarchy — not just a label.

87%
🟢 Fresh
51%–100% freshness · Color: --fresh-primary (#2EA44F)
Copy: "Fresh · X days remaining" · No pulse animation · Full opacity
28%
🟡 Expiring Soon
11%–50% freshness · Color: --fresh-warning (#FFB300)
Copy: "Expiring Soon · X hrs left" · Pulse animation on badge · Discounted pricing hint
4%
🔴 Critical / Expired
0%–10% freshness · Color: --fresh-danger (#E63946)
Copy: "Expired" · Add to cart disabled · Product shown with reduced opacity

F — ReferenceDesign Rationale

Reference 1

Why this style matches perishable-goods ecommerce

Perishable goods require decision speed. Unlike fashion or electronics where browsing is leisurely, a buyer deciding on bangus or kamatis needs freshness, price, and proximity in under 3 seconds. The dense-but-ordered card system, strong typographic hierarchy via Syne, and the three-state freshness color model are all designed for fast scanning — not exploration. The bold personality signals to buyers that this is a place that takes freshness seriously, building the trust layer that wet market vendors earn through familiarity.

Reference 2

How proximity and freshness are visually prioritized

Proximity (km) and freshness (%) are displayed in the product card body above the price line — breaking the conventional ecommerce hierarchy where price is primary. The freshness bar uses animated fill on mount, making it the first dynamic element the eye sees. Distance is shown with a map-pin icon alongside the seller name, so location feels like a persistent truth about the product, not an optional detail. On the buyer home screen, the nearest products appear first, and the search radius slider is the only filter shown by default.

Reference 3

How the design scales across buyer, seller, and rider workflows

The shared token system (fonts, colors, radius, shadows) ensures visual coherence across all three roles. Role differentiation happens at the navigation and data layer: buyers see search, freshness, and cart; sellers see analytics dashboards, inventory bars, and order management tables; riders see map-first job cards, distance, and earnings. Bottom nav tabs are role-adaptive — the same component reads from a role prop and renders the correct tabs. This means design QA, dark mode, and accessibility work is written once and applied to all roles.
